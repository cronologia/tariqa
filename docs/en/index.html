Chronology of the Maryamiyya and the Traditionalist School (1886–present)

About

This is a work-in-progress dataset about a semi-private initiatic order and, in part, living people. It is compiled only from published scholarship (Mark Sedgwick's work is the academic backbone) and participants' own public writings; sympathetic, critical and apostate sources are labeled as such; contested characterizations are attributed, never asserted in the site's own voice; dates the sources dispute are flagged with a ? mark; and no private individuals are named. Corrections against published sources are welcome.

What it is
A Sufi order (tariqa) of Western origin founded by Frithjof Schuon, and the organizational heart of the Traditionalist School — the perennialist current of René Guénon, Schuon and Ananda Coomaraswamy.[13] [14]
Lineage
A branch of the Alawiyya of Shaykh Ahmad al-Alawi (Mostaganem, Algeria), where Schuon was initiated in the early 1930s; Schuon assumed the function of independent shaykh in Basel in late 1936 — an investiture his followers describe as divinely confirmed and Sedgwick treats as self-authorization. Sedgwick (14 July 2010) places the order twice over. Organizationally it is, in his words, “an independent branch of the Alawiyya, which is itself an independent branch of the Darqawiyya, which is itself an independent branch of the Shadhiliyya” — “independent” meaning that it operates independently of the tariqa it derives from and that its practice, prayers and teachings differ in certain respects, which he notes is true of most tariqas in existence today. Personally, Schuon is “a link in a chain (silsila)” passing through Ahmad al-Alawi, Muhammad al-Arabi al-Darqawi and Abu’l-Hasan al-Shadhili, and then through both Ali ibn Abi Talib and Abu Bakr to the Prophet Muhammad.[13] [11] [1] [59] [60]
The name
'Maryamiyya' — after the Virgin Mary (Maryam), whom Schuon regarded as the order's spiritual protectress following visions he reported in 1965; the name was formally added in 1969 (full style: Tariqa Shadhiliyya-Darqawiyya-Alawiyya-Maryamiyya).[13]
Headquarters
Lausanne, Switzerland (1941–1980), then Bloomington, Indiana (1980–1998); since Schuon's retirement and death, autonomous zawiyas under individual khalifas — commonly described as Washington (Nasr) and London (Lings) branches, though Wikipedia's account stresses that no successor was named.[13] [17] [16]
In Brazil
A Brazilian branch founded in 1986/87 (sources differ) — described as the first tariqa brought to Brazil by Brazilians — with Olavo de Carvalho as Schuon's muqaddam until his break with Schuon at the end of the 1980s; centers reported at São José dos Campos (SP) with branch affiliations still to be verified. ?[4] [15]
Why it is in this project family
The documented crossovers: Rama Coomaraswamy — per Sedgwick never a formal Maryamiyya member, but a personal follower of Schuon who led a Catholic group practicing dhikr-like prayer with Christian concepts; also an SSPX seminary professor and sedevacantist priest — bridges to the fsspx project; Olavo de Carvalho (Maryami muqaddam turned philosopher of the Brazilian right) bridges to the fsp project's critical literature.[32] [10] [4]

1986 1986 ? Bloomington / São Paulo, Brazil The Brazilian branch founded — Olavo de Carvalho leads a group of Brazilians to Bloomington, is initiated (taking the name Sidi Muhammad Ibrahim, per the critical documentary blog, corroborated in outline by Sedgwick), and is appointed muqaddam in Brazil under Martin Lings's guidance — described as the first tariqa brought to Brazil by Brazilians. Sources split between 1986 and 1987 for the founding: 1986 is the year the hostile Jardim series gives — and Carvalho's own first-person placing of himself in the United States in '86, below, is consistent with it — while Brazilian tertiary sources give 1987. Sedgwick's published abstract places the Brazilian episode only in the 1980s and 1990s without a year, and the full Aries 21:2 article remains the document expected to settle it. Sedgwick's later obituary notice (28 Jan 2022) calls him "briefly a Maryami and the khalifa of Frithjof Schuon in Brazil", where this dataset and the Brazilian sources say muqaddam; the two titles are distinct and the divergence is recorded, not resolved. Carvalho's own dated account, in the Curso Online de Filosofia, adds a fourth voice to this record without settling it. In aula 094 (12 February 2011) he said that as soon as he entered Schuon's tariqa he was immediately appointed its muqaddam — which he glossed as the organization's representative for Brazil — that he had not asked for the post and found the speed of the appointment odd, and that the order then passed its instructions to a different man, creating a conflict of authority he described as dishonest. In aula 033 (21 November 2009) he said that at the time of the essays collected in Universalidade e Abstração (1983) he was an aspirant who later became a member, that he wrote in the manner of the Études Traditionnelles circle in order to please the shaykh, and that he was accepted at the first attempt while others had been applying yearly for twelve years. His self-description as muqaddam agrees with this dataset and the Brazilian sources and not with Sedgwick's obituary notice, which says khalifa; a participant's self-disclosure does not adjudicate a scholarly source, and the divergence stays recorded rather than resolved. Two further dated lectures bear on how he came into the order. In aula 206 (1 June 2013) he said that in '86 he was in the United States for the first time and that he met Whitall Perry there — a first-person placing of himself in the country in the year the Jardim series gives, although in that passage he named neither Bloomington nor the initiation. In aula 279 (17 January 2015) he described the route in: he reached Schuon's tariqa first through the Buddhist author Marco Pallis, and then Rama P. Coomaraswamy — whom he called a traditionalist Catholic theologian and Schuon's second-closest collaborator — put him in contact with it; his stated reason for joining was that the order was multi-confessional, so that belonging to it would not commit him to another religion. His own entry is self-disclosure and is accepted as such; what he says there about Pallis and about Coomaraswamy is a claim about third parties, carried attributed and, in Coomaraswamy's case, against Sedgwick rather than with him. None of this is quoted: the corpus text is a community transcription unchecked against the audio.[4] [29] [15] [5] [67] [62] [75] [78]
1989 1989 ? São Paulo, Brazil Olavo breaks with Schuon — Olavo de Carvalho leaves the order after a rupture with Schuon; the sources disagree on the date — 1987 (Bunker da Cultura), 'the late 1980s' (Sedgwick), or an expelled-1987–91 window (the Jardim series) — and on the reasons, with his own published framing (the multiconfessional rule, in 'Influências discretas') differing from the hostile accounts; every version is attributed. He reinvents himself, in Sedgwick's phrase, as a Catholic-identifying 'post-Traditionalist'. Carvalho's own tellings in the Curso Online de Filosofia are a fourth attributed account, and they diverge from each other, which is itself the finding. In aula 094 (12 February 2011) he framed the ending as his own act — when he broke with these people, he said, they activated the order's customary judicial harassment, inventing a case accusing him of a crime he had not committed which cost him six years and nearly ruined him before he won it; he added that the accusation he suffered came at forty (he was born in 1947), and that all of it was more than twenty years earlier. In aula 218 (31 August 2013), reading out a critic's summary that had him expelled from the Maryamiyya, he said that this checked out perfectly, that he had already explained the circumstances, and that he holds the whole correspondence; in the same lecture he said that one cannot leave a tariqa of one's own accord — either the man puts you out or you never leave. In aula 241 (1 March 2014) he repeated the six-year lawsuit and said the order's usual practice was judicial harassment, which it could not press further in Brazil because it had only eight disciples there. A third dated telling, in aula 242 (15 March 2014), supplies a cause the 2013 one did not: he said he had left expelled from the tariqa for quarrelling with the shaykh, and in the same passage that the order he had belonged to was Schuon's, which hated Freemasonry and put out anyone who went near it. That passage also appears to give the length of his membership as a year and a half — a figure that does not sit easily with an entry in 1986 and a break at the end of the 1980s — but the sentence is elliptical in a transcription never checked against the audio, and it is recorded here as an open question rather than as a duration. In aula 279 (17 January 2015) he said that Schuon, late in life and under pressure from other tariqas, decreed that only Muslims would be accepted: a dated restatement of the multiconfessional rule that his own published account gives as his reason for leaving, although in the lecture he neither dates the decree nor ties it to his own departure. Break-by-his-own-act and expulsion are not the same claim, and he made both; the year remains unfixed and the dateVerified flag stays down. Nothing here is quoted — the corpus text has not been checked against the audio.[4] [31] [29] [35] [67] [69] [71] [77] [78]

1991 1991-10-15/11-20 Bloomington (Monroe County), Indiana, USA The Bloomington affair — indictment and dismissal — A grand jury indicts Schuon, 84, on allegations by a former member concerning ritual gatherings; the judge criticizes the indictment as vague (Bloomington press coverage of 29 October 1991), and on 20 November the county prosecutor dismisses all charges, stating there was insufficient evidence — 'not one shred of evidence' beyond the complainant's allegations, per the wording quoted by Wikipedia — adding that a miscarriage had occurred insofar as Schuon had been labeled. Schuon was never tried and the case was never revived. In announcing the dismissal, prosecutor Robert Miller said the deputy prosecutor who had headed the grand jury investigation, David Hunter, 'has submitted his resignation' and had resigned his position 'last week', and declined to say whether he had requested it; Hunter for his part blamed Miller (contemporaneous Bloomington coverage as reproduced by accuratenews.net, a pro-Schuon archive, labeled as such). Critical accounts instead say Hunter was fired; both versions are attributed. One hostile ex-member's view of the allegation is on the record twice and is not what might be expected: in the Curso Online de Filosofia, aula 094 (12 February 2011) and again in aula 241 (1 March 2014), Carvalho — who in the same breath declares his own resentment against Schuon and describes a lawsuit the order's disciples brought against him — said he does not believe the pedophilia accusation, and in aula 241 attributed it to a man who had had an affair with one of Schuon's wives, was expelled, and wrote violently against him. He is an interested witness whose account of the complainant is unverified here and is recorded as his claim, not as a finding; it is noted because it comes from the order's critic, not its defenders.[11] [28] [9] [67] [71]

1997 1997 Lausanne / France Borella publishes his critique of Guénon and Schuon — Jean Borella publishes 'Ésotérisme guénonien et mystère chrétien' (L'Âge d'Homme, coll. Delphica; English translation 'Guénonian Esoterism and Christian Mystery', Sophia Perennis, 2004) — his public critique of Guénon's thesis on the Christian sacraments and of Schuon's supra-confessional esoterism. 1998 1998-05-05 Bloomington, Indiana, USA Death of Frithjof Schuon — The order continues in autonomous branches. The identification of the Washington branch with Seyyed Hossein Nasr is supported in Mark Sedgwick's own voice: his post of 16 January 2023 reports Nasr as the Shaykh of the Washington, DC branch of the order, authorizing a new independent Washington–Baltimore branch. The parallel identification of a London branch with Martin Lings circulates but is not supported by the Wikipedia article on Lings, which describes him as Schuon's disciple and expositor only; that identification is carried here as unconfirmed (to verify). Both identifications coexist with the no-named-successor account: per the Maryamiyya article, Schuon had resigned as shaykh in 1992 without naming a successor, each zawiya's muqaddam becoming an independent khalifa. A fourth account exists and disagrees with the no-named-successor framing: in the Curso Online de Filosofia, aula 041 (16 January 2010), Carvalho said that when Schuon died Martin Lings became shaykh of the tariqa shortly afterwards, and described the office in strong terms — a shaykh has no friends, only disciples. In aula 209 (29 June 2013) he gave a different emphasis, saying Schuon's tariqa dissolved after his death amid scandals and quarrels and that several tariqas emerged from it, the principal one being, in his account, of strict Islamic observance despite the universalist language of its discourse. Both are his attributed characterizations; neither is corroborated here, and the Wikipedia-sourced account above is not displaced by them.[11] [13] [48] [64] [68]

The silsila upstream of Schuon, as Sedgwick describes it (2010)

Mark Sedgwick, 14 July 2010: “in personal terms” Schuon is “a link in a chain (silsila)” passing through Ahmad al-Alawi, Muhammad al-Arabi al-Darqawi and Abu’l-Hasan al-Shadhili, “and then through both Ali ibn Abi Talib and Abu Bakr to the Prophet Muhammad, and thence to God” — the last step is stated in the node detail rather than drawn, because the chain runs through the two of them and not through one. Sedgwick adds that “the question of whether or not (or how) Schuon was authorized as a muqaddam by al-Alawi has no bearing or impact on this.” Organizationally he places the same descent as Maryamiyya < Alawiyya < Darqawiyya < Shadhiliyya, each an independent branch of the one before.[59]

Key figures

René Guénon (Abd al-Wahid Yahya)

1886–1951 · France / Egypt

Founder of the Traditionalist School; French metaphysician, Cairo-based Sufi from 1930; backed Vâlsan's 1950 separation from Schuon's branch.[12]

Frithjof Schuon (Isa Nur ad-Din Ahmad)

1907–1998 · Switzerland / USA

Founder and shaykh of the branch that became the Maryamiyya (1936–1992); leading perennialist author ('The Transcendent Unity of Religions').[11]

Ananda K. Coomaraswamy

1877–1947 · Ceylon / UK / USA

Art historian (Boston Museum of Fine Arts); with Guénon and Schuon one of the school's three founding authors. Biographical dates verified: 22 August 1877 (Colombo) – 9 September 1947 (Needham, Massachusetts).[14] [80]

Rama P. Coomaraswamy

1929–2006 · USA

Son of Ananda; cardiac surgeon, later psychiatrist; professor of ecclesiastical history at the SSPX's St. Thomas Aquinas seminary at Ridgefield, Connecticut 'for five years' — that is, until about 1983 — per the 2007 memorial by William Stoddart and Mateus Soares de Azevedo, which says nothing further about his part in the Society's internal disputes. The stronger claim in circulation, that he drew the future SSPV founders to sedevacantism, rests on a mirror of a since-deleted Wikipedia article alone and is recorded here as unverified (to verify) — Fr. Anthony Cekada's own account of the 1983 split does not mention him; the fsspx project carries that dispute. Sedevacantist priest ordained in 1999 by Bishop José Ramón López-Gastón of the Thục line (per his own published account); contributor to the Foundation for Traditional Studies' journal Sophia. Per Sedgwick (Against the Modern World, p. 161) he was never a formal Maryamiyya member but a personal follower of Schuon who led a Catholic group practicing dhikr-like repetitive prayer with Christian concepts — see the fsspx project's dossier. In Olavo de Carvalho's Curso Online de Filosofia, aula 279 (17 January 2015), he is called a traditionalist Catholic theologian and Schuon's second-closest collaborator, and Carvalho says it was he who put him in contact with the tariqa. That is an interested third party's characterization; it does not displace Sedgwick, and the same speaker said in aula 169 (25 August 2012) that Schuon had several right arms, like an octopus. Recorded as his attributed claim and raised with the fsspx dossier rather than adopted.[32] [27] [25] [33] [10] [26] [78] [73]

Titus Burckhardt (Ibrahim Izz ad-Din)

1908–1984 · Switzerland

Swiss author on sacred art and civilization; Schuon's closest lifelong collaborator. Asked in Olavo de Carvalho's Curso Online de Filosofia, aula 156 (26 May 2012), about Burckhardt's conversion to Islam, Carvalho answered that Burckhardt never converted, because nobody in a tariqa's elite converts and the notion has no place at that level, and that Schuon was Burckhardt's shaykh. The first half sits against this dataset's own record of Burckhardt under a Muslim name and is carried as his attributed claim, not as a correction.[20] [72]

Martin Lings (Abu Bakr Siraj ad-Din)

1909–2005 · England

British Arabist and Shakespeare scholar, author of the celebrated 'Muhammad' (1983); head of the order's London line; advised the founding of the Brazilian branch. Seyyed Hossein Nasr

b. 1933 · Iran / USA

University Professor of Islamic Studies at George Washington University (from 1984); founder of the Foundation for Traditional Studies; head of the order's Washington line; the school's most prominent academic voice. Carvalho met him and assessed him twice, differently, in the Curso Online de Filosofia: in aula 228 (16 November 2013) he described Nasr's Iranian upbringing and MIT training with admiration and said that on meeting him he found not only great learning but an intrinsic nobility of presence he had rarely seen; in aula 241 (1 March 2014) he called Nasr's history-of-science work wholly disinterested and his expositions of Sufism faithful, while judging his later books ideological propaganda for Schuon. Michel Vâlsan (Mustafa Abd al-Aziz)

1907–1974 · Romania / France

Romanian diplomat and esoterist; led the Paris Alawiyya and separated it from Schuon's branch in 1950 with Guénon's approval, founding the sharia-observant 'Guénonian-orthodox' line; editor of Études Traditionnelles. Biographical dates verified: 1 February 1907 (Brăila) – 26 November 1974 (Antony); the Wikipedia article is internally inconsistent about his age at death, but not about the dates themselves. Carvalho's account of the same split, in the Curso Online de Filosofia, aula 209 (29 June 2013), is consistent with this in outline and adds a name: he said the tariqas that had formed divided in two, between 'guénonistas' and the 'perenialistas' who followed Schuon, that the Guénonian half closed its orders to non-Muslims and required full observance of the sharia, and that roughly half of them were led above all by Vâlsan — a Romanian — and afterwards by Charles-André Gilis. Gilis is not otherwise documented in this dataset; the succession is recorded as Carvalho's attributed claim. Jean Borella

b. 21 May 1930 · France

French Catholic philosopher; professor of metaphysics and ancient/medieval philosophy at the University of Nancy (Nancy-II) from 1977 until his 1995 retirement. Tariqa Maryamiyya

Founded 1936 / named 1969 · Basel → Lausanne → Bloomington

Schuon's branch: independent from 1936, Marian in devotion from 1965, formally 'Maryamiyya' from 1969; headquartered in Lausanne 1941–1980 and Bloomington 1980–1998; decentralized after Schuon's retirement into autonomous zawiyas.[13] [76]

One ex-member's dated account of life inside the order, recorded as his attributed testimony and corroborated nowhere here: in the Curso Online de Filosofia, aula 220 (14 September 2013), Olavo de Carvalho described belonging to a tariqa as complete passivity before the shaykh — the disciple, whom he said the order calls fakir, being in the master's hands as a corpse is in the hands of the one who washes it — and gave two rules: one may enter but not leave, and one leaves only by expulsion. He gave Schuon's practice as his example: that Schuon decided what furniture a member kept at home, how many cigarettes a day he might smoke and how he should dress, and that the order was socially flat, having only everybody and the shaykh with some intermediate officers between. He is a self-declared resentful ex-member describing a semi-private order from the inside, and this is his characterization, not a finding; the same lecture makes claims about named public figures that this project locates and does not adopt (see the disambiguation section).

Disambiguation & nuance

This subject sits at the crossing of three different things called 'traditionalism', a semi-private religious order, and living people. The entries below keep the wires uncrossed; every characterization is attributed.

Traditionalist School ≠ Catholic traditionalism

The Guénon–Schuon school (perennialism) is unrelated in origin to Catholic traditionalism (Lefebvre, Écône, sedevacantism — the fsspx project's subject). The documented bridges are individuals: Rama Coomaraswamy, Jean Borella, and Olavo de Carvalho. Guénon himself began among French Catholic intellectuals and broke with them ('Orient et Occident', 1924).[14] [10] [21]

≠ Evola's political 'Traditionalism'

Julius Evola adapted Guénon toward radical-right politics; Schuon's line was religious-initiatic, not political. The later Bannon/Dugin/alt-right 'Traditionalism' story connects to the Evolian reading — Sedgwick notes Olavo de Carvalho 'applied Guénon to political issues rather as Evola had, but unlike Evola combined Traditionalism with Roman Catholicism'. Attribute all such lineage claims.[4] [14]

Maryamiyya ≠ mainstream Sufism

Criticism of Schuon's universalist adaptations began inside the Guénonian world itself — the 1950 Vâlsan split over the relaxation of Islamic practice — and continued over the Marian devotion, the Plains Indian rites and the Bloomington gatherings. Defenders present these as legitimate esoteric adaptation; critics as syncretism. Both framings are attributed. On the narrower question of “validity”, Sedgwick (14 July 2010) argues the Maryamiyya can be placed like most tariqas — organizationally within the Alawiyya–Darqawiyya–Shadhiliyya descent, personally through Schuon's silsila — and concludes that “the Maryamiyya is probably ‘valid’ to the extent that Schuon’s vision of the Virgin Mary in 1965 was ‘valid.’” The formula is conditional by design and endorses neither side; it is quoted here as written.[1] [9] [13] [59]

Academic perennialism vs initiatic membership

Perennialism was taught openly in universities (Nasr, Smith) while tariqa membership remained private. This site keeps the published-scholarship layer separate from claims about membership, and carries no information about private living members — deceased figures and self-disclosed members only.[17] [7]

The 1991 Bloomington affair carries its resolution

Any mention of the 15 October 1991 indictment must carry the 20 November 1991 dismissal of all charges, the prosecutor's insufficient-evidence statements, and the fact that Schuon was never tried and the case never revived. The chronology's single paired entry is the pattern to follow.[11] [28]

Names and nicknames

'Guru de Virgínia' is a Brazilian press nickname for Olavo de Carvalho (from his Virginia residence, 2005–2022), not a title about Schuon. The order's full style is Tariqa Shadhiliyya-Darqawiyya-Alawiyya-Maryamiyya; 'Maryamiyya' alone is standard in scholarship.[15] [13]

Follower ≠ member: the 'Christian Schuonians'

Sedgwick documents a category the record keeps flattening: Christians who followed Schuon personally without ever joining the tariqa — Rama Coomaraswamy's American Catholic group ('none of these Christians were actually members of the Maryamiyya'), probably Jean Borella's French circle, and the Orthodox scholar James Cutsinger, Schuon's editor, 'never (as an Orthodox Christian) a formal member'. Membership claims about individuals require self-disclosure or scholarship — adjacency is not initiation. One participant's dated account describes both the arrangement and its closing, and is carried attributed rather than as corroboration: in the Curso Online de Filosofia, aula 156 (26 May 2012), Olavo de Carvalho said Schuon directed Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant and Buddhist groups and moved people between them at will, and in aula 279 (17 January 2015) that the order was multi-confessional when he joined and that Schuon, late in life and under pressure from other tariqas, decreed that only Muslims would be accepted. The same speaker, in aula 242 (15 March 2014), named two prominent Christian clergymen — a Spanish Catholic priest and an Orthodox bishop — as members of the tariqa. That is a third party's assertion about named public figures by a self-declared resentful ex-member, and it is the very move this entry exists to resist, because it flattens the follower/member distinction Sedgwick draws. It is located here and deliberately not carried as a membership record.[32] [37] [38] [72] [78] [77]

Brazil's Guénonian scene today (attributed to Felipe Souza, 2023)

A 2023 guest post by Felipe Souza on Mark Sedgwick's Traditionalists blog — authored by Souza, not Sedgwick — maps a Portuguese-language Guénonian milieu: the René Guénon Institute for Traditional Studies (IRGET), run by the late Luiz Pontual de Oliveira; translator-teachers Marcelo Cipolla and the História Islâmica portal (Mansur Peixoto); and Luiz Gonzaga de Carvalho, Olavo's son, said to attend the classes. These roles rest on Souza's single account and are recorded as attributed, not confirmed. Souza also situates Nova Resistência (Raphael Machado) as a Duginist movement that 'flirts with Guénonism' — that is Evola/Dugin-line political Traditionalism (the 'Fourth Political Theory'), kept apart from the Schuon school per this project's three-traditionalisms rule; it is not the Maryamiyya's initiatic line.[36]

Olavo de Carvalho on his own tariqa years — a participant-witness whose tellings diverge

Between 2009 and 2014 Olavo de Carvalho talked about the Maryamiyya repeatedly in his Curso Online de Filosofia, and the vaulted corpus of that course (cronologia/archive, ADR-0005) lets those statements be cited by lecture number and lecture date rather than through a hostile channel's re-cut. Two rules govern how they are used here. First, he is a participant-witness on his own involvement and an attributed, interested voice on everyone else: in aula 218 (31 August 2013) he confirmed that he had belonged to Schuon's tariqa and that a critic's statement that he was expelled from it checked out perfectly, and in aula 094 (12 February 2011) that he was appointed muqaddam for Brazil as soon as he entered — self-disclosure, which this project accepts for his own membership. His characterizations of Nasr, Lings, Vâlsan, Borella and Upton are a different kind of claim and are carried attributed, beside the published scholarship, never in place of it. Second, his account does not adjudicate anything. On the date of the break, his own tellings do not even agree with each other: in 2011 he described leaving as his own act and dated the accusation that followed to his fortieth year, while in 2013 he accepted the word 'expelled'; that divergence is a fact about the source, and the 1986/87 founding and the end-of-the-1980s break stay flagged. On his title, his self-description as muqaddam agrees with the Brazilian sources and not with Sedgwick's obituary notice, which says khalifa — recorded as a divergence, not settled by a participant's word against a scholar's. One earlier datum belongs here too: in aula 019 (15 August 2009) he said he had come across Guénon's books in the 1970s and had read them all by about 1981 or 1982, which places his reading years before any of the dates above; the frequently repeated line in that same lecture claiming eyewitness standing among the tariqas is not his — it belongs to a student's letter he reads aloud, and is attributed here to that correspondent. A later pass through the 2012–2015 lectures adds three things to this picture. The eyewitness claim does exist in his own voice, in a different lecture: in aula 171 (8 September 2012) he said that direct experience of Islamic esotericism through Schuon had happened to very few people and had given him a privileged observation post, and that he had watched history from it as an eyewitness. What does not drift is the order's structure: aula 156 (26 May 2012), aula 242 (15 March 2014) and aula 279 (17 January 2015) agree that Schuon's was a multi-confessional order and that he belonged to it — it is the ending, and only the ending, that he narrates differently each time. And the same lectures carry claims about other people that this project locates and does not adopt: in aula 220 (14 September 2013) a member of the British royal family and a former Swedish prime minister are named among the disciples of Schuon's disciples, and in aula 242 two named Christian clergymen are called members of the tariqa. A participant's word is self-disclosure about himself and an accusation about anyone else. One reliability caution belongs beside them: aula 220 puts Schuon's return to Europe announcing that he would islamize it in the 1950s, where this dataset has him back from Mostaganem in the 1930s and the order in Lausanne from 1941 — the same loose dating of other people's lives already noted at Martin Lings. Two further limits are worth stating plainly. Eighteen of the lectures behind this section carry a date in the corpus manifest, none of them among the fourteen headers archive#20 found inconsistent with their neighbours; several others that discuss the order at length carry none, because the corpus's unreviewed half has no headers to read a date from, and an identification without a date is a partial result that is not cited as a claim. And none of the wording is quoted anywhere in this dataset: the transcriptions are a community effort that has not been checked against the audio, so everything attributed to these lectures is paraphrase.[67] [69] [71] [62] [64] [68] [5] [4] [61] [74] [72] [76] [77] [78] [75] [73]
